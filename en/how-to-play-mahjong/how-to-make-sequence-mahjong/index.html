How to Make a Sequence in Mahjong: The Set That Only Comes From the Left

Three tiles in a row, one suit, no gap between them. Sequences are the easiest set to build in mahjong and the only one the table will not hand you from any seat.

What counts as a sequence

Three consecutive tiles of the same suit, no gap in the middle. It is the only meld in mahjong whose tiles are all different from each other, which makes it the easiest one to spot in a thirteen-tile hand.

The range runs from 1-2-3 up to 7-8-9. That is seven per suit and twenty-one in the set, and none of them wraps around: 8-9-1 is not a chow. The 9 ends the line, it does not start the next one.

Winds, dragons, flowers and season tiles sit out entirely. They only pung, kong or pair, so a hand stacked with honors never closes on sequences.

The physical count behind each meld explains the gap. A 1-2-3 of bamboo can be assembled in 64 different ways, since every tile in it has four copies in the set. A pung of 5 of circles exists in exactly four: pick three of the four copies. Same three tiles in the hand, sixteen times the supply.

Only the seat on your left can feed it

Pung and kong may be called off any seat. A chow may not. You can only take the discard from the player immediately on your left, the one who played just before you, because turns run counterclockwise and theirs is the only discard that passes through your hand.

That changes how you read the table. If the player on your left throws the 3 of characters you need, the chow is sitting in front of you. If the same 3 comes off the far side of the table, you wait and say nothing.

The claimed tile lies sideways in front of you, turned to point at whoever threw it. Someone walking up mid-game can rebuild part of the hand from those angled tiles.

The American league is the exception. Any seat may feed you there, and the trade-off is a different rule: a joker may not be used in a run, only in a pung, kong or quint. Players who try to finish a run with a joker find out when they call it.

Three waits, three counts

Two tiles waiting on a third get named three different ways, and each way draws from a different number of tiles.

  • Two-sided (3-4). Completes on a 2 or a 5: up to eight copies in the set.
  • Closed middle (4-6). Only the 5 works, and you are holding no 5: four copies.
  • Edge (1-2 or 8-9). Only the 3 or only the 7: four copies, with the sequence pinned to the end of the suit.

Eight against four is double, and the gap shows up in the third round, once two of those copies are already in the public pile. Under riichi, the closed middle and the edge add 2 fu over the two-sided wait, and most players still take the wide wait: those 2 fu vanish inside ten-point rounding, and only the two-sided version keeps pinfu alive.

What an open hand costs

Calling a chow off a discard puts the meld on the table and opens the hand. Under riichi that bill arrives all at once: no riichi, no menzen tsumo, no pinfu, no iipeikou, since all four require a closed hand. Scoring shifts too, with a closed chow worth 4 fu and an open one worth 2.

In Hong Kong an open chow scores no fan on its own. A hand of nothing but chows plus a pair is the chicken hand, paid at the table minimum even with all fourteen tiles perfectly in place.

What you buy is tempo. The call leaves the hand one tile closer to finished and pulls the tile you used out of the public pile. In a hand that is already open, another chow costs almost nothing. In a closed hand, every call shuts a scoring door.

Why a 5 beats a 1

A 1 lives in one chow: 1-2-3. A 9 lives in one: 7-8-9. A 2 lives in two. A 5 lives in three, at 3-4-5, 4-5-6 and 5-6-7.

Hence the discard order most beginners ignore: 1s and 9s early, 2s and 8s next, a 5 only when nothing else is left to throw. The 5 is the most connected tile in the set, and the last one you part with for no reason.

The rule flips once it is built. With 1-2-3 already down, the 1 is no longer a tile, it is a meld, and the question changes: not "what can this tile still become" but "which sequence is this hand still missing". The step-by-step rules cover that switch partway through a hand.

Where sequences do not exist

Sichuan plays with 108 tiles, the three numbered suits only, and has no chows at all. The whole table runs on pungs, kongs and the pair, which inverts the value of a 1 and a 9: with no runs available, a 1 is as useful as a 5.

The games on this site work on yet another logic. Solitaire runs on pairs, and so does Connect: two matching tiles leave the board and a three-tile meld never forms. Sequences only exist in Classic Mahjong, with all 144 tiles and four real players.

Which tile leaves when the chow stalls

For a player who sits down once a week: hold the 5, cut 1s and 9s early, and call a chow only when it closes a meld the wall would not hand you in two draws. Otherwise let the tile go and keep the hand closed.

The exception is a hand that needs points rather than speed. If the plan is pinfu, iipeikou or riichi, no chow call goes in, because an open hand is worth less than the same hand closed and one tile of progress does not cover the difference.
