Mahjong Myths and Legends: Seven Stories the Table Still Repeats

Someone throws a 3 of bamboo and mentions that the suit comes from the 108 outlaws of Water Margin. Three players nod, the hand moves on, and the tile set gets no defence.

The 108 outlaws who never fit in the set

The story told most often inside China is not the Confucius one, which we settled in who invented mahjong. It is this: the 108 suited tiles stand for the 108 outlaws of Water Margin.

The number lands neatly and the arithmetic fails. A 144-tile set holds 108 suited tiles, but only 27 different faces, four copies of each. A deck built from heroes would need 108 distinct faces. What exists is the opposite: three suits of nine numbers, each repeated four times, because four is what allows a pung, a kong and a pair on the same table.

The suits came from somewhere else, and the names still say it. The character suit counts myriads, the old unit for strings of coin. The bamboo suit is a string of a thousand coins with the hole in the middle. The circle is the coin. All three inherited their denominations from paper money games played long before tiles existed, and the 108 came out of the structure of the game rather than out of a novel.

The sparrow on the one of bamboo

The Chinese name of the game is maque, sparrow. The version heard at tables says the tiles sound like sparrows in a tree when they are washed, and that the nickname grew from there.

The sound is charming and explains nothing, since the name was on the decks before the tile game existed. What holds the sparrow up is a face: the one of bamboo shows no bamboo at all. It shows a bird perched on a branch, and on many sets that bird is a sparrow. It is the only tile in the suit with an animal on it.

Worth keeping the distinction, because it comes up in club arguments. The name comes from the bird on the tile. The noise at the table is a result, not a cause.

The eight flowers are not the eight immortals

Plenty of older sets engrave the eight Taoist immortals on the back of the bonus tiles. From that decoration comes a line you will hear: "the eight flowers are the eight immortals".

The faces disagree. Four plants, plum, orchid, bamboo and chrysanthemum, and four seasons spelled out. The immortals sit on the reverse, where no rule ever looks.

What the story hides is the part that costs points. A flower pays only when it belongs to you: Hong Kong scoring gives 1 fan for the flower matching your seat and another for the matching season. Chinese official scoring gives the tile no value on its own; it only buys a replacement draw from the tail of the wall. Japanese sets leave the eight out entirely, since riichi runs 136 tiles. The detail sits in our guide to flowers and seasons.

The hot tile and a memory that keeps bad books

Every table has one. "Don't throw the five of circles, it keeps coming back." The feeling is real. The mechanics are not.

The wall is sealed before the first draw and does not reorder itself because somebody asked. In a 144-tile set, 53 tiles leave in the deal, fourteen to the dealer and thirteen to each of the other three, and 91 stay in the wall. With 136, the wall holds 83, fourteen of which become the dead wall and are never drawn, leaving 69 live draws.

Memory does the rest. You file the two nights a discarded tile came back and paid, and never file the twenty when it stayed buried. One hard rule covers a tile coming back: furiten. A tile in your own discard pile cannot complete your hand. Everything else is counting what is visible.

On a screen the books get honest. Our Solitaire has no wall and no draw: all 112 tiles are face up from the start, and the only thing between you and a cleared board is the order in which you open space.

Ivory, bone and the joint that gives it away

An old set that looks like ivory almost never is. The standard late-19th-century build is a slice of ox bone about two millimetres thick, dovetailed onto a bamboo back. Such a set weighs close to 4 kg and clicks in a way plastic does not.

Real ivory did exist in expensive export sets, and since 1989 the international trade has been banned under CITES. The fastest test is the back: bone and bamboo are two pieces joined, seam visible. Plastic comes out of a mould in one piece.

"It was banned in China" is half true

After 1949 mahjong was attacked as a gambling habit and a feudal leftover. Sets went into hiding, some were destroyed, production stopped. There was no single decree with a date and a clause. There was a campaign, and a campaign is not a statute.

The return came in the 1980s, with production restarting and tea houses reopening. Chengdu now runs halls with hundreds of tables, playing the 108-tile game with no chows and a compulsory void suit. The game that came back was not the one that left.

The lucky seat

Swap chairs, blow on the dice, refuse the north seat. The rule already handles seat luck on its own: the dealer starts as East, and when the dealer fails to win, East passes to the player on the right and the winds travel with it. Across four rounds everybody deals once. The dice do not pick a winner. They pick where the wall opens.

What to do with the story

Don't correct anyone mid-hand. The story is part of the evening, and the player who opens with the 108 outlaws is making conversation, not asking for a lecture.

Three of the stories change a decision, and those deserve a sentence before the first draw: whether flowers pay, whether a tile can run hot, whether the seat matters. Agree the rule set out loud and the argument ends there.

The exception is a club or a tournament. A printed rule sheet exists there, and "everyone plays it this way" is worth nothing. If you want the beliefs that actually move a discard, the piece on mahjong and superstition covers them, and the Culture index holds the rest.
