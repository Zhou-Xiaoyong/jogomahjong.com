Mahjong Rules Step by Step: Complete Beginner's Guide

Want to play Mahjong without locking the board on the third move? This guide explains the rules step by step — from the goal to the free-tile rule — so you can play your first match with confidence.

Why it's worth learning the rules before you click

Mahjong looks like a Chinese puzzle full of odd symbols, but it hides a simple logic. Players who learn the rules first understand each move in seconds; those who don't click at random and get stuck on the third move. This guide breaks the essentials into short steps so you can play with confidence from the very first game. If you'd rather start from absolute zero, our beginner's guide covers the same ground at a slower pace.

First: there are two "Mahjongs"

The phrase "Mahjong rules" can mean very different things, and mixing them up is the most common mistake newcomers make:

  • Classic Mahjong — the four-player table game with turns, draws, discards and scoring. The original, centuries-old game.
  • Mahjong Solitaire — the one-player puzzle you'll find on this site: match identical free tiles from a stacked board until it's empty.

The tiles are identical in both, but the rules differ a lot. This article focuses on Solitaire, because that's where everyone should start — and it's the version that runs free in your browser, no download.

The goal of the game

One goal only: empty the board. You do it by removing tiles two at a time, whenever they match and are free. When no tile remains, you win.

  • Every tile on the board comes in a pair (two identical copies).
  • Clicking two identical, free tiles makes them vanish.
  • The challenge isn't finding pairs, it's finding them in the right order — because removing one tile frees others but can trap the rest.

The tiles in brief (you'll recognize them in 2 games)

A full set has 144 tiles, but they fall into a few categories:

Numbered suits (1 to 9)

  • Circles (筒): discs that look like old coins.
  • Bamboos (索): green sticks; the "1 of bamboo" is usually a little bird.
  • Characters (萬): Chinese number ideograms.

Honor tiles

  • Winds: East, South, West and North.
  • Dragons: red (中), green (發) and white.

Flowers and seasons

Eight special tiles with a privilege: in Solitaire, any flower matches any flower and any season matches any season. They don't have to be identical — and that's where many players waste moves for nothing.

The rule that decides the game: you can only remove a free tile. A tile is free when (1) nothing covers it from above and (2) its left or right side is clear. A tile in the middle of a row stays blocked until its neighbor leaves.

Mahjong rules step by step

With the goal and the free-tile rule in hand, the game becomes mechanical. Follow these steps:

  • Step 1 — Read the board. Before the first click, see where the free tiles are (edges and tops).
  • Step 2 — Pick a pair. Two identical tiles, both free.
  • Step 3 — Click one, then the other. If they're right, they disappear together.
  • Step 4 — Re-check what opened. Each removal frees tiles underneath.
  • Step 5 — Repeat. Back to step 1 until the board is empty.
  • Step 6 — If stuck, use the tool. Hint, reshuffle or restart — all part of the game.

The best way to lock in the rules is to practice: open free Mahjong Solitaire in another tab and follow the steps with the board in front of you. Ten minutes is enough for the logic to become automatic.

What about the 4-player classic rules?

In the original game, each player starts with 13 tiles and tries to build four combinations (pungs or chows) plus a pair, drawing from the wall and discarding one tile per turn. The first to complete a hand wins. The tiles are exactly the ones above — the dynamics change, not the set. For the backstory of where it all came from, the complete how-to-play guide lists every mode on the site, from Connect to Pyramid.

Mistakes the rules prevent

  • Clicking the first pair that appears. Three identical tiles on the board? Picking the wrong two can block the tile you'll need at the end.
  • Ignoring flowers and seasons. Getting stuck looking for the exact design when any flower would do is the most common slip.
  • Confusing similar circles. The 6, 8 and 9 look alike; count the dots calmly.
  • Giving up at the first dead end. Running out of moves is normal early on — that's what reshuffle is for.

Quick questions about the rules

Do I need to memorize all 144 tiles?

No. You only need to spot identical pairs, and that happens visually after two games. The suit names help you talk about the game, but they aren't required to play.

Can I remove two different tiles if no pair exists?

No. In Solitaire only identical pairs count (except flowers-with-flowers and seasons-with-seasons). The four-player classic uses a different combination logic, but that's outside solitaire.

Does every board have a solution?

Not always. Random boards can genuinely lock up, which is why the game offers hints and reshuffle. Treating a dead end as part of learning takes the pressure off.

Next steps

To sum up: identical tiles make pairs, only free tiles leave, and the board must end empty. That's literally all of it. The rest is practice and patience. Start with a small board, raise the difficulty slowly, and explore the other modes once you feel comfortable. Have fun!
