How to Play Mahjong Pyramid: Layers, Blocked Tiles and How to Win

Mahjong Pyramid stacks 60 tiles into four levels, and one rule drives every move: a tile only leaves the board when nothing sits on top of it and at least one side is open. Here is the full mechanic, plus the clearing order that empties the table.

What Sets Pyramid Apart from Standard Solitaire

Pyramid is a solitaire, though not the 144-tile turtle layout most players picture. This board is a four-level ziggurat holding 60 tiles, which gives you 30 pairs to clear. The win condition never changes, but the route to it does.

Because the stack is vertical, the top row is the only group guaranteed to be playable on move one. Opening from the base first usually burns moves on pairs that were sitting there anyway. The Four Layers, by the Numbers

Each level is centred over the one below it. A 10 px offset produces the staircase look you see on screen.

  • Base (level 0): 8 columns by 4 rows, 32 tiles.
  • Second level: 6 by 3, 18 tiles.
  • Third level: 4 by 2, 8 tiles.
  • Top: 2 tiles side by side.

The deck draws on the core set only: four winds, three dragons and the three numbered suits from 1 to 9. Flowers and seasons stay out, so the familiar "any flower matches any flower" shortcut never applies on this board.

One detail saves real time. Thirty pairs cannot cover all 34 faces of the core set: characters and bamboos run the full 1 to 9, but circles stop at 5. Hunting for a 7 of circles means hunting a tile that was never dealt.

What Counts as a Free Tile

Two tests, both required.

  • Nothing on top: no tile on a higher level may share its column and row.
  • One open side: at least one immediate neighbour, left or right on the same level, has to be gone.

Blocking is computed on the grid, not on the artwork. That 10 px offset makes tiles look partly overlapped, yet a level 1 tile covers exactly one cell on level 0. In practice the ends of each row are almost always playable, the top is too, and the middle of the base only opens once the layer above it clears. A covered tile ignores your click, and nothing about the cursor warns you.

How a Pair Leaves the Board

Click the first tile, then its twin. Both have to be free.

  • Identical faces pair up. Different suits never do, even at the same number: a 5 of bamboo will not take a 5 of circles.
  • Clicking the selected tile again cancels the selection and costs no move.
  • A wrong pair shakes the tile, adds a move and leaves the new tile selected.

That last point wrecks most move counts. Clicking quickly without checking the face turns 30 pairs into 60 or 70 moves. Reading has a trap of its own: the 1 of bamboo is drawn as a bird, not a stick, which catches nearly everyone in the first few rounds.

Counters and Buttons

Moves, pairs (12/30 and so on) and a clock sit above the board. The clock starts when the page loads, not on your first click, so a tab left open already carries a fat time before you sit down.

  • New game: reshuffles all 60 tiles and resets every counter.
  • Hint: highlights a playable pair. No penalty, no usage limit.
  • Shuffle: swaps the faces of the remaining tiles and keeps each one where it stands.

Here is the difference that catches players arriving from another mode: with no playable pair left, the pyramid does not reshuffle itself. It tells you and waits for the button. Mahjong Chain handles that situation automatically. Pyramid does not.

A Clearing Order That Works

Work downward, one horizontal band at a time.

  • Take the two top tiles first. Each one frees the matching cell on the third level.
  • Finish a row before starting another. On the base, clear from the ends toward the middle. Every tile you remove opens its neighbour, so a row becomes fully available without touching anything else.
  • Lean on Hint. It costs a second of reading, nothing more. Guessing pairs to see what sticks costs moves.
  • Save Shuffle for the end. It only re-deals the faces still on the table, so using it with 20 pairs left buys you two moves before the block returns.

Pyramid, Solitaire and Chain

Mahjong Solitaire also stacks tiles in layers, but the classic layout carries 144 tiles in a much wider fixed shape. Pyramid is smaller, symmetrical and quicker to read. Connect drops layers entirely: tiles sit in a flat grid and a pair only leaves when a path of at most two turns links them. Chain keeps that path rule and adds a 4.5 second clock between combos.

Three Mistakes That Stall a Board

  • Shuffling at the first block. If tiles remain on upper levels, the block is temporary. Climb before you shuffle.
  • Ignoring the pair counter. 8/30 with the base untouched means the top tiles are still sitting there.
  • Waiting for a flower. There are none on this board, so no wildcard will rescue an orphan tile.

Where to Start

With ten minutes to spare, clear the pyramid top-down in horizontal bands and keep your hands off Shuffle until 20/30. The exception comes when the two top tiles belong to the suits you keep mixing up. Open one base row first, get your eye calibrated, then return to the top with a steadier hand.

Open free Mahjong Pyramid and take the two top tiles first. If speed is what you want, Chain wraps up in a two-minute round.
