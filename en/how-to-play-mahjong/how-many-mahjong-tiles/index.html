How Many Tiles in Mahjong: The Count That Changes Mid-Hand

The question looks simple until you count the wall after dealing. Between the closed box and the last draw, the tile count on the table changes four times, and each change answers to a different rule.

The box says 144. The table never shows 144.

Build four walls from a complete set and count stacks instead of tiles: 72 stacks of two, 18 to a side. Each seat gets 36 tiles in front of it, the tidy figure everyone quotes. It holds for about two minutes.

The moment the deal ends, the number on the table is something else. Anyone counting loose tiles loses the thread during the second round, because the wall is not one pile. It is four stretches shrinking at different rates.

Fifty-three tiles leave before the first discard

A classic deal hands out four tiles at a time, three passes around, then one more tile each: thirteen per seat, 52 tiles in total, or 26 whole stacks. The dealer then takes a fourteenth tile off the top of the next stack, and that is where the symmetry breaks. The stack stays half used.

Ninety-one tiles are left, and 91 does not divide by four. That leftover of three is why the last pass around the table is never even.

Ninety-one tiles is 45 stacks and one loose tile

What remains is 45 full stacks plus the single tile from the stack the dealer opened. Count the stacks after dealing and get 46, count again: one of them holds a single tile.

At a table where nobody claims a discard, each seat draws about 22 times before the wall runs dry. Claims pull that figure down, since the player who takes a discard does not draw on that turn. Most hands end well before the wall does.

The eight flowers draw before the game starts

The 144 include eight bonus tiles, four flowers and four seasons, and none of them stay in your hand. Show one, park it beside the wall, draw again, and keep going until thirteen numbered tiles are in front of you.

So the first draw of the game can happen before anyone discards. A player holding three flowers has drawn sixteen tiles to keep thirteen, and the wall is three tiles shorter than the earlier arithmetic predicted. At a table with bonus tiles that error is small, and those few tiles decide who draws the last one.

The Japanese table sets fourteen tiles aside

A Japanese set holds 136 tiles, eight fewer, which is why its walls run 17 stacks to a side. Before the deal, fourteen tiles come out as a reserve and sit face down at the end of the wall.

That leaves 69 draws for the whole hand against 91 at a Chinese table. Someone arriving from a 144 set sits down at 136 and wonders why the wall dies so early. It is not a feeling: 22 fewer tiles are in circulation.

Counting the wall buys a decision, not a number

Count the stacks at the moment you begin waiting on one specific tile. Six stacks left means twelve draws for four players, three apiece. Waiting for the last copy of a face from there is betting on something with almost no time to happen.

At twenty stacks the same wait is reasonable. The count guarantees nothing, but it changes what you throw away now. That is the line between chasing a pair and taking the pung already in your hand.

On a screen the count is a design choice

A digital board owes nothing to 144. This site's Solitaire builds 112 tiles, 56 pairs, from 34 faces with no flowers at all. Blitz runs 76 tiles across three layers, Memory uses 36, and Slide works with 12 queue slots and no wall whatsoever.

So the 91-in-the-wall figure is useless in every one of them. The pair count still behaves: 112 tiles give 56 pairs, 76 gives 38, 36 gives 18. Read the counter on screen and halve it to know how much is left.

When the count fails at the end of a hand

Add up everything on the table once a hand is over: the four hands, the exposed melds, the discards, the wall. The total has to come back to 144. A total of 143 means a tile hit the floor or slid under a discard.

With one tile missing, the next hand starts crooked, because somebody will wait on the fourth copy of a face that no longer exists. Club sets lose tiles this way, roughly one a year, and nobody notices until a hand stalls.

What to do with the number

Count stacks before you sit down, not tiles: 18 to a side for a 144 set. It is faster, and a wrong wall shows at a glance.

The exception is a Japanese set, which wants 17 stacks to a side plus the fourteen-tile reserve kept apart. Build 18 from 136 tiles and the wall comes up short.
