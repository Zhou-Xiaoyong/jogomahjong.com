How to Make a Pair in Mahjong: The Eye That Closes a 14-Tile Hand

Fourteen tiles sit in your hand, twelve of them already spoken for by four groups of three. The leftover two form the only two-tile set the game allows, and that pair decides whether the hand closes.

The only two in a game of threes

Fourteen tiles, twelve of them locked into four groups of three. The two at the end make the only two-tile set mahjong permits, and English-speaking players call it the eye.

Every table runs the same arithmetic. Japanese riichi, Hong Kong mahjong, the American league card: 4 × 3 + 2 = 14. Hold thirteen tiles and you are always one away, and that last tile can be any design at all as long as it completes the pair.

Japanese players call it jantou, sparrow's head. Melds are the body; twelve well-arranged tiles without a head score nothing.

Which sets up the first trap, and it is structural. A pair cannot be split. Send one of its two tiles into a chow and the eye is gone; you need a fresh one before you can declare. Solitaire makes that obvious, since there the pair is the entire rule: matching tiles leave the table and no hand gets completed at all.

No call brings you a pair

You can claim another player's discard to finish a pung, a chow, or a kong. There is no call that finishes a pair. That single gap separates the pair from every other set in the hand.

Put it on the table. You hold a lone 7 of bamboo and want its twin. Somebody discards a 7. That discard works for the player holding 8 and 9, works for the player holding two 7s who wants a pung, and does nothing for you. Your tile arrives two ways only: off the wall, or as the tile you win on.

That makes the pair wait the quietest in the game: it needs nobody and uses nothing the table hands you. The how-to-play index splits the hand into stages, and the pair turns up in nearly all of them.

Calling the third tile costs you the eye

Two 5 of circles sit apart as your likely eye. A third 5 hits the discard pile on your right. Calling pung looks like free progress: one closed meld and one less tile in the public pile.

Except those two tiles now form a pung. The pair is gone. If the hand was two tiles from finished, pair complete and one meld half built, the call converts that into an extra meld plus an entire pair still to find.

Call when the pung solves a meld you could not assemble any other way, or when another pair in the hand already does the head's job better. Otherwise let the 5 go by. The step-by-step rules list the three legal calls, and the pair is not among them.

A pair wait takes three tiles

The mirror case shows up more often: four melds done and one tile stranded, waiting for its partner. Japanese players call the wait tanki, and the count is easy. You hold one copy of a design, so three copies remain in play.

Set that against a two-sided wait, say 3-4 of bamboo taking 2 and 5: up to eight copies. Three against eight is the gap between finishing a hand and watching the wall shrink.

Riichi throws in 2 fu for the pair wait, and here is the honest part: it almost never moves the total, because fu rounds up in tens and the gain disappears inside the rounding. The real value of tanki is flexibility. You can swap the stranded tile for a different one mid-hand, which a two-sided wait never lets you do. Beginners feel that before they know its name, and the beginner walkthrough shows where it first bites.

Pairs do not all score alike

A dragon pair and a wind pair are not the same animal on the scoresheet. Under riichi, a pair of dragons is 2 fu, a pair of the round wind or your seat wind is 2 fu, and a pair of a wind that is neither counts zero. Three pairs that look identical, three different values.

Hong Kong counts in fan, and houses split on this one. Some give 1 fan for the dragon pair alone, others only score the pung. Ask before the first hand; the answer changes what you cut in the fourth round.

The American league card is blunt about it: jokers may not be used in a pair. Whatever pair the hand calls for has to be natural tiles, which is why cards that look simple stop so many players on the first try.

Seven pairs means seven different pairs

One hand turns the pair from head into the whole structure: seven pairs, 2 han, a flat 25 fu, and no tile called off the table.

The seven have to be distinct. Four of a kind does not count as two pairs, and that is the mistake that follows the hand's name around. A quad is one pair with two tiles left over; one of them goes to the discard pile, and anyone counting a quad twice thinks they are closer to finished than they are.

Which pair leaves first

Two pairs mid-hand, no meld closed, one tile must go. The choice is not between the pretty pair and the ugly one. It is between the pair holding useful tiles and the pair holding nothing.

A pair of 4 of characters ties up two tiles that would also work in 3-4-5 and 4-5-6. A wind pair ties up nothing, because no chow uses a wind. While the hand is still open, the suited pair leaves and the honor pair stays as the likely eye.

The exception is a tile the table is throwing away. A pair of 4 of circles with two 4s already in the public pile is the cheapest pung on the board, since the third comes from any seat. Then the suited pair stays and the wind pair goes.

If this is still theory, play a hand of Classic Mahjong before sitting down with four people. Same 144 tiles, same three real calls, no money and no clock.
