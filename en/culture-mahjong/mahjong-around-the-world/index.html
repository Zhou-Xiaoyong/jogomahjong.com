How Mahjong Spread Around the World, and Why Every Country Changed the Rules

The tiles crossed the ocean more or less unchanged. The rules did not. The same 144-tile game shows up in New York with 152, in Tokyo with 136 and in Chengdu with 108, and every cut has a reason behind it.

What crossed the ocean was a rulebook

In 1920 an American engineer living in Shanghai, Joseph Park Babcock, published a small rulebook in English. The tiles had been around for more than half a century by then. What left China that year was one table's reading of the game: the mix of Cantonese and Shanghai habits that foreigners played in the Shanghai concessions.

Babcock trademarked the hyphenated spelling, Mah-Jongg. Abercrombie & Fitch in New York sold the set with the manual inside the box. Anyone learning in Ohio learned a version that had already been filtered.

The pattern repeats at every port. A game arrives as a sealed package, and the first local generation never sees the variants left behind. Two years later, that single reading is "how the game is played".

The American craze lasted two winters

In 1923 the United States bought sets by the shipload. Clubs opened in New York, Chicago and San Francisco, with afternoon tournaments and entry fees. By 1924 the stock was stuck in warehouses and importers dumped it at cost.

The game did not die with the fad. It moved into living rooms and community centers, and that is where the American version grew rules of its own, far from China and far from any new manual.

New York dropped the runs and added eight jokers

An American set holds 152 tiles: the traditional 144 plus eight jokers. There is no chow at all. No runs, only pungs and pairs, and that shifts the whole calculation: without runs, the middle tiles of a suit lose value and discards get cruder.

Nobody counts fan. The table works from a card. The National Mah Jongg League, founded in 1937, prints a new list of hands every April, and a hand only counts if it appears on that year's card. Dozens of patterns, none of them negotiable.

The opening exchange is local too: the Charleston, three passes of three tiles, right, across, left. No other school does it.

Tokyo removed the flowers and gained information

The Japanese set runs 136 tiles: the 144 minus the eight flowers and seasons, which never made it into competition play. Those eight tiles are not a saving on material. They make room for the dead wall, fourteen tiles nobody draws from and the place the dora indicator comes from.

Riichi is a Japanese invention, not a Chinese one. You declare that you are one tile from winning, stake 1,000 points, and play the rest of the hand frozen until you close it or someone collects. The federation that standardized play in Japan dates to 1929.

So the wall becomes a display board. The dora indicator is public, it changes the value of every hand at the table, and it forces a fresh calculation each round. Neither Hong Kong nor Sichuan has that layer.

Sichuan cut 36 tiles so a game fits an afternoon

Chengdu plays with 108 tiles: three suits, nine ranks, no winds, no dragons, no flowers. Before the first discard each player names the suit they are abandoning and has to shed it first. With no honors to sit on, hands close in a few turns.

The rule that trips up visitors: the round does not end at the first win. The winner leaves, the rest keep playing until one player is left. The local name, xué zhàn dào dǐ, battle to the end, sums it up.

Hong Kong was the one that cut nothing

The arithmetic lands on 144: 108 suited tiles, 16 winds, 12 dragons, 8 flowers and seasons. The Cantonese version, played in Hong Kong and exported to the rest of the world, kept all four parts. No jokers, no card, and scoring counted in fan, tile by tile, once the hand is closed.

The cut there is elsewhere. Most tables require 3 fan before a hand counts, which sets a floor: a 1-fan hand simply does not close. Learn in Hong Kong and you learn to build value, not to fill in a pattern.

In 1998 China tried to fit it all in one book

The State Sports Commission published the Competition Rules: 81 hand patterns and an 8-point minimum before a win counts. The plan was a single book any country could sit down with and play, no arguments.

The side effect is that local flavor became a score sheet. A hand that closes and pays in Chengdu can be worth zero at a tournament because it never reaches 8 points. The international rule is not the most played anywhere, and that is what it is for.

Brazil got the game twice

First in 1812, with the Chinese workers brought to Rio to grow tea. Then from 1908, with Japanese immigration, which settled around Liberdade in São Paulo. For decades mahjong stayed inside those communities, played at home and in neighborhood halls.

The screen is what opened it to everyone else. Mahjong Solitaire here lays out 112 tiles in 56 pairs across 34 faces, with no seat wind, no dora and no opponent's discard pile. What is left is the core: find a free pair, keep a column from closing.

The full timeline from 1850 sits in History of Mahjong. The years when the game spread across imperial China are in Mahjong in the Qing Dynasty, and the Culture index collects the rest.

Which deck to start with

To learn scoring, start with the 144-tile Hong Kong set. It is what guides, videos and club tables use, and the only one that teaches fan without an annual card.

The exception is a group that refuses to count. Then 108 tiles from Sichuan ends a round in twenty minutes, and solitaire handles the rest on its own.
