Mahjong and Women in China: The Table They Kept for Themselves

A 1935 Shanghai film has a middle-class mother playing mahjong in the parlor while her son burns with fever in the next room. She ends the scene by throwing the set out the window.

Two tables with a wall between them

Through the last decades of the empire and into the early Republic, both sexes played mahjong. They rarely played at the same table. A screen stood between the two games. Each table drew from one family, and the partners invited to play with Cixi, the dowager empress, were consorts and palace princesses.

The point was not manners. It was containment. A woman spending the afternoon at a closed table among relatives is still inside the house. One who leaves the house to play is on the street.

The reformers got scared

After 1912 the arithmetic changed. Reformers saw the modern wife in heels at a tile table instead of minding her children, and read it as the whole problem with a modernizing country.

Film recorded the panic. In Little Angel (1935), directed by Wu Yonggang, the neighbors have the big house and a mahjong set in the corner of the parlor. That set is the first sign the household has rotted. The mother plays all day, the husband stays out at night, and the son gets sick with nobody watching. She ends the scene by throwing the tiles out the window.

Read the gesture backwards and something else shows up. That woman had an activity of her own, with rules, a schedule and other women in it: the first mass pastime a respectable housewife took for herself, which is why it became the symbol of decay.

The turn to speak has an order

The table gave something the household did not always give: the floor, at a fixed time. Play moves counterclockwise, one player at a time, and nobody takes two turns in a row. Whoever is up draws, discards, decides. The other three wait.

Small thing. It repeats all afternoon, and it does not care about family rank. The daughter-in-law who says nothing over dinner says plenty at the table, because the table never asks how old she is.

Three short of one

The game needs four players and will not run on three. Chinese has a phrase for that gap, san que yi, "three missing one." It works as an invitation and as a demand. Whoever has a table and three players starts calling, and that is how mahjong created an obligation for women that ran around husbands and children.

When the fourth does not show, somebody from outside sits down. That's the hinge of Lust, Caution (2007), Ang Lee's film: it rains, the player who would have completed the table stays home, and one of the husbands takes the chair.

Two seven of circles

What happens there is not only about points. The man sits to Wang Jiazhi's left, which under the rules makes him the one who can feed her hand, since a chow can only be called from your left. He discards a seven of circles. She moves to take it and the hostess pungs it first. He gets the message and throws a second one minutes later.

Two identical discards in one hand, at a table where nobody repeats a tile by accident, is a message. The scene is about an affair and about politics, and the carrier is the rule: the player on the left holds the power to serve.

1949: the table leaves the parlor

After 1949 mahjong was classed as gambling and as a feudal leftover. Sets went into hiding, some were destroyed, and the table disappeared from living rooms for decades. It came back in the 1980s, when tile production restarted and halls opened in the cities.

The table that returned was a different one: it had moved outdoors. Tea, bamboo chairs and noise became part of the game, and the woman playing no longer had to hide the set behind a screen.

The Sichuan rule that stretches an afternoon

In Chengdu and Chongqing the standard table runs on 108 tiles: three suits, no winds, no dragons. Three rules hold that table together for an afternoon.

First, there is no chow. Nobody calls a tile off the neighbor; you pung and you kong. Second, each player picks a suit to abandon before the first draw, the que yi men, and has to empty it by the end. Finish the hand still holding tiles from that suit and you are a "flower pig," hua zhu, paying the full price.

Third is the rule the game is named for, xue zhan dao di, "fight to the end." When someone completes a hand, nobody stands up. The winner leaves and the other three keep playing. Then another one leaves, the table drops to two, and whoever never wins pays all of them.

Hong Kong runs the other way: four rounds, and the game ends when the last wind passes. A table that does not stop when somebody wins is built to last.

Two tables today

Two of them. The afternoon one sits in a tea house or a neighborhood club: retired women, small stakes, a session that ends when the school gate opens. The daughters' table is a phone, and the other three are rarely in the room, but it's still where you talk about what the family does not ask.

What to do with this at a real table

To feel how a rule shapes a gathering, play one afternoon of Sichuan: 108 tiles, no chow, a suit to abandon, nobody getting up when somebody wins. Next to the 144-tile Classic, hands close faster and the table stays.

The exception is a group of three. Then the table becomes solitaire, and Solitaire covers it: 112 tiles in 56 pairs. What it cannot give back is the part that kept this game alive among women for a century: the turn to speak, the san que yi call, the fourth chair that is always empty.
