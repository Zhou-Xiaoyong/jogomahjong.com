Mahjong in Famous Movies: What the Table Does in Each Genre

A 1984 Japanese film opens with a kid losing everything at a tile table and spends the next 109 minutes trying to get back to one. There the game is not party decoration. It is rent.

The film where the table pays the rent

The Tokyo of Mahjong Hōrōki (1984) is still rubble. The kid arrives with train fare, sits down at a gambling table and loses all of it the first night. The next 109 minutes are his climb back to a rematch, and the camera barely leaves the square.

Makoto Wada made his directing debut on it, adapting Takehiro Irokawa's autobiographical novel. Irokawa hustled tiles before he wrote books. Black and white settles a practical problem: with 136 tiles lying still, the eye needs to separate the hand from the face across from it, and color gets in the way.

Nobody in this film plays for affection. They play to eat. A loan shark, an occupation officer, a cheat and a kid who still believes in luck take the four seats.

The New Year comedy turns the table into a character test

Fat Choi Spirit opened in Hong Kong on February 8, 2002, in Lunar New Year week, directed by Johnnie To and Wai Ka-fai. The title is the holiday greeting that asks for prosperity, and the film took HK$19.2 million giving the audience what the holiday wanted.

Its argument is blunt. Andy Lau plays a compulsive player who refuses to marry his girlfriend because she flips the table when she loses. He is not grading her game. He is grading what she does with a loss.

His brother is the mirror: enormous luck, zero skill. The film keeps returning to that gap because the rules contain it. Luck decides which tile reaches you. Skill decides what you do with the thirteen already in your hand, and nobody can lend you that.

The third genre is the tournament

Mahjong Dragon (1997) and The King of Mahjong (2015) move the table into a championship. A hand becomes a punch, a discard becomes a bluff, and the score has to be announced out loud so the crowd knows who is ahead.

The game loses its patience in these films. One hand runs five to ten minutes and a full round of sixteen hands eats the night. The edit room solves what the table will not.

Thirteen in hand, fourteen on your turn

Here is the check any viewer can run from the couch. A player waits with thirteen tiles. The fourteenth is the one just drawn, and it either closes the hand or goes face up into the discard row.

East is the exception. The dealer opens with fourteen and discards without drawing. A scene where all four sit still holding fourteen tiles is a continuity error, and it turns up more often than you would guess.

The three-fan floor

Almost every table filmed in Hong Kong plays to a three-fan minimum before anyone can declare a win. Four chows and a pair, the easiest shape to build, scores zero. Complete is not the same as legal.

That changes what a scene means. Screen winners almost always win with a hand the rules would refuse, and the audience has no way to tell. The floor is also what makes the game about decisions: sometimes holding a dead tile beats feeding the player on your left.

Who actually pays

Win on your own draw and all three opponents pay. Win on a discard and the player who threw that tile covers the whole hand alone, while the other two keep their wallets shut. East pays and collects double.

So the discard is the most expensive move at the table, which is exactly the move cinema treats as a gesture. Throwing away the tile that would have finished your own hand does not just cost the hand. It buys someone else's, out of your pocket.

Where the discards sit

Each player stacks their own discards in front of them, in rows of six, face up. Four separate rows, not one pile in the middle of the table.

It reads like a props detail and it is not. Those rows are the game's memory, the one thing all four players are reading the whole time. A central pot removes from the viewer precisely what the table is staring at.

The list that includes a film with no tiles

Circulating lists of mahjong movies include Indecent Proposal (1993). There is not one tile in it. The wager happens on a card table in a Las Vegas casino.

The mistake has its own logic. What mahjong carries is the idea of betting the house, and that anxiety simply moved tables. Worth knowing before you sit down expecting the clatter.

One case where the seat matters

The Joy Luck Club (1993) usually gets summarized as the daughter taking her dead mother's place at the table. The scene is sharper than the summary. In the first game after the funeral, Lindo holds East, Ying-ying South, An-mei West, and Jing-mei sits North.

North plays last, the seat of someone who has not dealt anything yet. The daughter does not inherit command of the table. She joins the rotation and waits her turn, and the book needs four blocks of four stories before she understands what that seat was saying.

Picking a film for tonight

For the table as work and pressure, Mahjong Hōrōki. For the table as character, Fat Choi Spirit. Neither explains a rule, and both are better for it.

The exception is anyone who wants to learn the game. Film cannot teach mahjong, because the cut always lands on the slow part. That is what Solitaire is for: 112 tiles in 56 pairs, so you can learn the faces with no opponent and no clock. The closest thing to a four-handed table is Classic, with 144 tiles.
