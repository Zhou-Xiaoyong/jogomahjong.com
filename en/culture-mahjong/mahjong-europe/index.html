Mahjong in Europe: Why a Complete Hand Can Score Nothing

You close four pungs and a pair at a club night in Portugal and the hand gets waved off. It is complete, it counts six, and nothing under eight can be declared in Europe.

Four pungs, a pair, six points

You close four pungs and a pair at a club night in Portugal. The hand sits there, finished, and the player on your left counts it out loud. Six points. You cannot declare it.

That is not a house rule or a veteran being difficult. European competition runs on one shared rulebook, and the rulebook wants eight points before any win counts. Below eight the hand is real and worthless.

The eight-point floor, and the pung that closes the gap

Scoring adds named patterns, each with a fixed value. Four pungs and a pair make All Pungs, worth 6. A dragon pung is worth 2. One dragon pung anywhere in the hand finishes the math, which makes it the shortest route to a legal win.

A pung of 1s or 9s is worth 1. Six plus one is seven, and the hand stays illegal. That single point knocks down more beginners than any discard rule.

Flowers and seasons score 1 each and sit outside the floor. Draw all eight and you can still be stuck at six.

There is one escape hatch, and it has a name: Chicken Hand, eight points for a hand that closes no pattern at all. It will not rescue a hand already scoring six, because the pattern is there and the bonus stays out.

Nobody buys a card

Europe's first decision was to skip the annual card. The rulebook is public and it never expires in April. The body behind it is the European Mahjong Association, founded on June 25, 2005, during the first European championship in Nijmegen, Netherlands.

Seven countries signed on: Austria, Denmark, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy and the Netherlands. The association now counts 21 national organizations, and the only Portuguese-speaking one is Portugal's.

It is registered in Denmark and owns no playing hall. A calendar of certified tournaments, a rulebook and a ranking list. That is the whole operation.

144 tiles and no dead wall

A competition set holds 144 tiles: 108 suited, 28 honors, 8 bonus. A flower does not sit in your hand until the hand ends. You call it, take a replacement, and keep your turn.

The replacement comes off the back end of the wall, opposite the live draw. Here is where riichi players trip: this rulebook has no dead wall. The fourteen tiles Japan sets aside stay in play, so counting what is left works differently.

Chow is legal here

European play allows chow, three consecutive tiles of one suit taken off a discard. American players blink at this, because no line on their card accepts one.

The right is narrow. Only the player to the discarder's right may claim a tile for a chow. Pungs and kongs are open to everyone, and a winning claim always outranks them.

Who pays

Win on your own draw and all three opponents pay 8 plus the hand value. A 24-point hand collects 32 from each, 96 in total.

Win off a discard and the shape changes. The player who threw the tile pays 8 plus the value, and the other two pay a flat 8. Same 24-point hand: 32 from the feeder, 8 apiece from the rest.

Nothing multiplies. Because the math adds, feeding a big hand is expensive while staying out of it is cheap, and defensive discards are worth more than they look.

Sixteen hands to a match

A match is four rounds of four hands, with East rotating every round. Sixteen hands close it, and a tournament session is usually exactly that.

Under a clock, time decides. A championship weekend fits six sessions or more, and the winner tends to be the player who miscounts least, not the one who closes the prettiest hand.

One list across borders

A certified event in Prague and a national championship in Lyon feed the same table. That is how a club player ends up ranked beside a European champion, and the list has had a dedicated manager since 2005.

With no annual card, the ranking is the only thing that carries from one season to the next. It also explains why Europe treats mahjong as a sport with a calendar rather than a living-room gathering.

Two circuits, one association

Europe runs two championships side by side. The Chinese-rules event has been biennial since 2005. The riichi event started in 2008, with Japanese rules and 136 tiles.

The first European title went to a Japanese player, Masato Chiba. In 2011 Ildikó Hargitai of Hungary won, with a Dane second and a Dutch player third. In 2006 the continent had about half a dozen certified tournaments. By 2019 there were more than seventy, split evenly between the two rule sets.

Getting in

Find the club before you buy tiles. The usual door is an open night, no fee and no betting, with somebody walking you through the count on your first hand.

Learn the floor before you learn big hands. Four pungs plus one dragon pung reaches eight and teaches the whole mechanism in a single deal.

The exception is a player with no club nearby. A home table on Hong Kong rules covers it, and Mahjong Solitaire here handles the pair-matching half alone, 112 tiles in 56 pairs.
