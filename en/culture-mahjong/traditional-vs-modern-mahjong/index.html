Traditional and Modern Mahjong: Four Boxes, One Game

A Shanghai set from the 1920s holds 144 tiles. A Japanese set holds 136, an American set 152, a Chengdu set 108. The loop on the table is the same in all four, and that is where the comparison gets interesting.

Four boxes, four counts

The 144 comes out of a plain sum: 108 suited tiles (three suits of 36), 16 winds, 12 dragons and 8 flowers and seasons. That is the number that left Shanghai in the 1920s, packed in wooden cases with point sticks.

Japan dropped the eight flowers and kept 136. The subtraction paid for the dead wall, fourteen tiles stacked in seven pairs that refill every kong and hold the dora indicator before the first discard.

The United States went the other way and added eight jokers to the 144, landing on 152. A joker is not a free tile. It only counts inside a hand printed on that year's card.

Chengdu cut deeper: 108 tiles, three suits, no winds and no dragons. Hands close in half the time, and the table makes up for it with a rule where nobody stands up when somebody wins.

The floor: modern mahjong's real invention

This is the part that trips up anyone who learned at a kitchen table. In a family game, any closed hand counts. In modern rules, the hand needs a name.

Riichi calls it a yaku: at least one named pattern, every hand. Four dora sitting in your hand pay nothing without one, because dora multiplies a hand that already scores and never builds value from zero. The Chinese competition rules published in 1998 list 81 patterns and demand 8 points before you may declare. In the US, the combination has to appear on the national league's card, which is reissued each year, so a tidy hand that is not printed there cannot be called at all.

The floor is not tournament snobbery. It changes the decision. Without a floor you throw away what does not fit and wait. With one, you throw away a nearly finished hand because it has no name.

Two tells that give the version away

Sit at an American table and look at the corner of a suited tile. US sets print the Arabic numeral next to the character, so a player who does not read Chinese never has to memorize the circle order. Chinese and Japanese sets offer no such help.

The second tell is the opening. American mahjong starts with the Charleston, a compulsory pass of three tiles left, three across and three right before anyone draws. No Chinese or Japanese table does this, and the Charleston is the first shock for anyone switching rule sets.

The third is furniture. American play usually puts a rack with a pusher in front of each player and stands the tiles on edge, facing the owner. At an Asian table the tiles lie flat on felt and the wall goes up by hand.

Sticks became a chart

An old set came with sticks worth 10, 100 and 500 points. The arithmetic happened out loud at the table, and each player checked the neighbour's count. Mistakes were routine. Nobody lost a hand over one.

Printed scoring charts came next, and the counting stopped being an argument. Now the app settles it before you finish looking at your tiles. What went missing was the social half of the math, the four people who disagree about twenty points and then agree on a number.

The table that talks, the table that doesn't

A home table is loud. Discards get announced by name, luck gets discussed, somebody complains about the wind. None of that is a rule. It is habit, and it changes from one neighbourhood to the next.

A competition table is quiet, and it charges for noise. In riichi, calling mahjong on an incomplete hand is chombo: the usual penalty is 8,000 points paid to the table on top of the lost hand.

That is the least visible change and the deepest one. The rule now exists to be enforced by a third party rather than negotiated among four people who know each other.

The mahjong that was never a table game

In 1981 Brodie Lockard wrote a game on the PLATO teaching system that used the same 144 tiles and none of the rules. No opponent, no draw, no discard: just matching pairs pulled off a stack as long as the tile is free on both sides.

That is the format most players outside Asia meet first, and it has no winds, no dragons and no runs. Our Solitaire runs 112 tiles in 56 pairs with the familiar faces. None of it is the four-player game, but the artwork is identical, which is why so many people sit down at a real table convinced they already know how to play.

What never moved

Four seats. Thirteen tiles in hand, fourteen on the draw. A wall that gets built and eaten from both ends. Draw, discard, wait.

One player at a time, counter-clockwise, and a run can only be claimed from the player on your left. That geometry predates 1900 and no modern version has touched it, because it is what makes the game work. Your left-hand neighbour can feed you. Your right-hand neighbour cannot.

Picking your table

If you are new and have four people, stay on the 144-tile game with no floor: the Classic set with flowers and seasons, token stakes and the count spoken out loud. Hands close quickly, mistakes are cheap and you learn to read discards without paying tuition.

The exception is anyone who wants to compete. Then reverse it: pick one rule set and stay there for six months. Riichi, Chinese competition and Sichuan reward different skills, and switching every week leaves you good at none. Japanese riichi is the easiest to study alone because an app will correct your hand on the spot. The American game is the hardest to learn without a patient neighbour, since the yearly card rewrites which hands exist.

What survives at both ends sits in the Culture index: the same wall, the same 34 faces and the same wait for a tile that may not come.
