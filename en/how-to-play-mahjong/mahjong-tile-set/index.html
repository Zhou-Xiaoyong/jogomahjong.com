Mahjong Tile Set Structure: Why Every Face Exists Four Times

A mahjong set is not a pile of identical pieces. It is three batches that repeat on different terms, and that difference decides which tiles can run, which can only pung, and which never enter your hand at all.

A set follows two rules of repetition, not one

A set holds 144 tiles and follows two rules of repetition. The 136 obey the first: every face turns up four times. The other eight obey the second, each appearing exactly once. Nothing in a traditional set escapes both.

The first rule covers 34 faces, 27 suited and 7 honors, and that is the playable deck. The second covers the four flowers and the four seasons.

The fourth copy belongs to the table

Three matching tiles make a pung. A fourth makes a kong. Since the set holds exactly four, two pungs of the same face cannot exist in one game: that would take six tiles, and six is more than four. Anyone who declares a kong removes that face from the other three players' pungs for the rest of the hand. That is a move, not trivia.

Counting is public information. Hold two copies and two are left somewhere between the wall and the other hands. Hold three and only one is unaccounted for. This is how a discard gets decided without guesswork. Bookkeeping, not luck.

Twenty-seven faces take an order. Seven take none.

A run needs a neighbour, so the 4 asks for the 3 or the 5. The 27 suited faces have neighbours because they are numbered 1 through 9 in three rows. Honors have neither number nor row. East does not sit west of South under any rule set, and a red dragon has no green dragon beside it.

That leaves 28 tiles, roughly one in five, that can only become a pung, a kong or a pair. Never a run. Holding a lone wind in the hope that it will shape up is holding a tile with nowhere to go.

The set offers 21 runs against 34 pungs

In a row of nine numbers a three-tile run can start at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 or 7. Starting at 8 closes nothing, because there is no 10. Seven runs per suit, 21 in the whole set.

Pungs work differently. Any of the 34 faces makes one, as soon as three copies meet. Add it up and the set allows 55 meld shapes, 21 runs and 34 pungs. A player who only sees runs is working with a little over a third of what the box offers.

Sorting thirteen tiles into those shapes is the drill of every hand, and the walkthrough sits in how to read mahjong tiles.

The batch that passes through your hand

Flowers and seasons are the only group with one copy per face. There is no second Plum, so they can never pair by matching. The rules handle that by treating the batch as one group rather than four distinct faces.

They also never stay in your hand. Draw a flower, show it, set it beside the wall, draw again. The tile takes no slot among your thirteen. It is the only part of the set that crosses the hand without joining it.

The rule survives every rule set

Variants change the modules, not the repetition. Japanese sets drop the eight bonus tiles and stop at 136. Sichuan goes further and drops honors entirely, leaving 108. American sets add eight jokers for 152. In all of them, every face still exists four times.

That matters before you buy anything. Learn the structure once and it applies at any table, because the copy count does not change with the country. What changes is which modules enter play.

On a screen the four-copy rule can break

A digital board has no obligation to respect a physical set, and Connect does not. It builds 80 tiles from 16 faces: the seven honors plus only the 1, the 5 and the 9 of each suit. The 2, 3, 4, 6, 7 and 8 are simply absent.

Copies stop being four as well. Eight faces appear six times and eight appear four, which is how the board reaches its 40 pairs. The subtraction you do at a real table does not survive here. Looking for a 6 Character in Connect means looking for a tile the mode never deals.

Checking a set in five minutes

Turn everything face up and sort by suit. Each nine-face suit should give nine piles of four, 36 tiles per suit. Honors give seven piles of four. Flowers and seasons give eight singles, one of each.

A pile of three means the set is incomplete, and the game stalls late, when someone waits on the fourth copy of a face that went missing years ago. Second-hand sets fail this way more often than not, and the seller rarely knows.

What to buy, and what to play first

Buy 144 with the flowers, then count the piles of four before the box goes on the shelf. After that open Solitaire, which runs on 112 tiles and 34 faces with no bonus tiles to slow the reading down.
