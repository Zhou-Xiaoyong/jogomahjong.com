How to Read Mahjong Tiles: Numeral, Suit, and the Tile Your Hand Needs

On a real table there is no caption under the tile. There is a two-centimeter drawing and three seconds to decide. Reading starts with the suit, moves to the number, and ends with the tile your hand is already asking for.

Thirteen tiles in hand, fourteen to win

A finished hand holds fourteen tiles: four melds of three plus a pair. You hold thirteen, so exactly one tile is missing, and that tile is not a matter of taste. It is whatever closes the shape already in front of you.

Sorting thirteen tiles into that shape is the skill. Naming them one at a time is only the entry fee.

On screen the caption does the work

Open Solitaire and every tile carries a caption in a strip at the bottom: 1 Character, 2 Circles, White Dragon. At 0.48rem on a desktop it reads fine. On a phone it drops to 0.38rem and turns into a hint rather than a label.

The plural is not consistent across suits, worth knowing before you trust it. Circles and characters pluralize, bamboo does not, so "2 Circles" and "2 Characters" sit next to "2 Bamboo". Screen readers announce the caption together with the original character, so a tile is never a mystery to someone who cannot see the face.

Suit first, number second

Every suited tile answers two questions in a fixed order: which suit, then which number. The three suits draw numerals in three unrelated systems, so the overall shape has to come before the detail. The full tour of the 34 faces, with counts and colors, sits in what are the mahjong tiles. This page picks up where that one stops: what to do with a tile once you have named it.

The character suit is the only one you read as text

Characters put a numeral on top and one repeated character underneath. The first three numerals are literal. One horizontal stroke is 1 (yī), two strokes is 2 (èr), three strokes is 3 (sān). No guessing involved.

Then the system stops being a stroke count. The 4 (sì) is a box with two marks inside, not four lines. The 5 (wǔ) is a top stroke, a vertical and a base, and counting its lines gets you nowhere. From 6 to 9 the shapes turn friendly again for anyone raised on Western digits: the 7 hooks like a seven, the 8 splays into two legs, the 9 folds over.

The bottom character changes with the set

The lower half repeats the character for ten thousand across all nine tiles. A traditional set draws it in twelve strokes, tall enough to fill most of the face. A simplified set uses a three-stroke version, which leaves the tile looking oddly empty.

A set bought in mainland China and one bought in Macau or Taiwan are not the same object, and a player raised on either pauses over the other. The numeral keeps its position either way.

The names a real table uses

Chinese players do not say character, bamboo and circle. They say wan, sou and pin, and the mapping is exact: wan for characters, sou for bamboo, pin for circles. Three words, one minute, and you stop floating in the conversation.

Honors run on different logic. No number at all. Four winds (East, South, West, North) and three dragons (red, green, white). None of them can join a run, and that single fact reshapes the whole hand.

Pull out what can never be a run

Start with the honors and count copies. A lone honor does nothing: it only scores as a pung or a pair, and the pair needs a copy you do not have. Two copies are a pair in waiting. Three are a pung already.

What remains are the suits. Within each one, order by number and look for gaps of one or two. Tiles three or more numbers apart ignore each other unless they match exactly.

The arithmetic of a wait

Every face exists four times. An open-ended run like 4 and 5 in one suit waits on the 3 or the 6, which is eight tiles in the set minus whatever is visible on the table or in your hand.

Other waits are thinner. A closed wait, 4 and 6 waiting on the 5, has four copies left. An edge wait, 1 and 2 waiting on the 3, also four. A pair waiting for its partner has two.

That gap decides discards. Between two tiles that look equally useful, keep the one opening the eight-tile wait.

Four tiles that defeat counting

  • 6 Circles and 9 Circles: the 6 stacks two rows of three, the 9 stacks three rows of three. Read the height of the pattern, not the dots.
  • 1 Bamboo: no stalks at all, just a bird. The only tile in the 144 with an animal drawn on it.
  • White Dragon: blank on a physical set. Position and context are the only clues.
  • 1 Circle: one large disc alone, impossible to confuse with anything else after the first time.

On a screen the order flips

With 112 tiles on the board nobody reads numbers one by one. Color resolves first: wind in blue, character and dragon in red, bamboo in green, circle in near black. Shape comes second, the number last.

The drill pays off because a physical table moves slower than a screen. Anyone who clears 112 tiles in forty seconds will not get lost in thirteen.

A five-minute drill

Deal thirteen tiles face up and split them into four melds plus a pair out loud. Three rounds is enough. By the third you name suits without thinking and start seeing the gaps.

Do this before sitting at a four-player table. The exception is the eight bonus tiles, flowers and seasons: they never count toward the thirteen and score as bonus only.
