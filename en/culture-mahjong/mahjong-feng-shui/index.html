Mahjong and Feng Shui: Where to Sit and What a Compass Really Measures

Someone always arrives early and refuses the chair with its back to the door. The choice has a name, a school and a compass behind it, and the first roll of the dice can wipe it out in four hands.

The chair nobody wants

In a mahjong room the worst seat is the one facing away from the door. You cannot see who walks in, you cannot see the exit, and you spend the evening turning your head at every sound in the hallway.

Feng shui has a name for this. The favorable spot is the diagonal opposite the entrance, with a solid wall behind you, and the chair backing onto the doorway is the one left without support. The rule was written for beds, dining tables and desks. Mahjong tables joined the list later, when the game became a teahouse habit in the late nineteenth century, and they joined by analogy.

Four directions come with the tiles

Sixteen of the 144 tiles are winds. Four directions, four copies each: east, south, west and north. No other tile in the set names a compass point, and that corner of the box is what gives feng shui something concrete to talk about.

Chinese tradition ties each direction to a season, an element, a color and a guardian animal. East is spring, wood, green and the azure dragon. South is summer, fire, red and the vermilion bird. West is autumn, metal, white and the white tiger. North is winter, water, black and the black tortoise. The center, which on the table is the bare space where discards land, is earth and yellow.

The table is over two thousand years old and was built to orient houses, graves and cities. Friction starts when it gets carried over to a game where each player's direction changes by rule.

Sitting with your back to the north

The most quoted orientation in Chinese architecture is to sit north and face south. The main halls of the Forbidden City open south, and in old houses the seat of honor is the one at the far end of the room, facing the door.

Moved to the table, the idea becomes club advice: take the chair facing south. Mahjong does not hand out seats by merit, though. The dealer is east, the other three take south, west and north in turn order, and the only thing a player picks is where to hang a coat.

The square table and the name it carries

A household mahjong table is square, one player to a side, and in most of China it is the same table the family eats at. The traditional name is the table of the eight immortals, because eight people fit, two per side.

The shape carries a cosmological reading: four sides, four seasons, four directions, one center. On the table the center is where discards go, and it is the one spot all four players can see at once.

What the compass measures

Anyone serious about feng shui uses the geomantic compass, with twenty-four directions on its outer ring. The reading is not taken at the table. It is taken at the front door of the building, with the facing measured there, and it applies to the whole home.

There is a doctrinal reason the table stays out of it. The school that organizes a dwelling starts from three fixed elements: the door, the main room and the stove. Furniture that gets dragged around does not enter that count, because the reading does not change when it moves.

Which leads somewhere practical. Shoving the table two meters to the left changes no direction in the house. What changes the evening is what sits behind each player, and that is comfort rather than calculation.

One round in every seat

The rules settle the geography without any help. The opening dealer sits east. Fail to win a hand as dealer and the deal moves one chair to your right, taking the east wind along, so the table rotates without anyone standing up. Four rounds in, every player has held all four seats and dealt four times.

Nobody stuck in the bad chair stays there all night. One round at most, and the table rotates without anyone standing up.

Riichi draws for it before the first tile: each player takes a wind tile and sits where it points. The dice only decide where the wall opens.

Sichuan never gets that far. The set has 108 tiles, no winds and no dragons, and not one tile in the box points anywhere. At a Chengdu table, seat feng shui is interval conversation.

What actually changes the evening

Three room arrangements have real consequences, and none of them needs a compass.

  • A wall behind each player. A chair backed against plaster keeps the household traffic and the hip-check out of the game.
  • No low beam over the table. A beam pulls your head down and cuts the light exactly where the tiles are.
  • Doors off everyone's back. If that is impossible, at least keep the door shut, since a draft takes light tiles off the table.

The third one has a playing consequence. A tile that hits the floor is a tile revealed, and a revealed tile is free information for the other three.

When tradition helps and when it wastes the night

For a home game, treat feng shui as room design. Table in the corner, wall behind, light from above, nobody facing a busy corridor. The hands run better and no one argues about a compass mid-hand.

Clubs and tournaments are the exception. Seats are drawn, a printed rule sheet exists, and a referee is standing there. Bringing the compass to a tournament table changes no wall, no dice and no hand. It changes the time you lose complaining about the chair.
