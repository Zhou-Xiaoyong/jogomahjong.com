Origin of Mahjong in China: Where and How the Game Was Born

The origin of mahjong in China is wrapped in legends, rival theories and a touch of mystery. In this article you'll meet the cities that claim to be the game's birthplace, the paper-card ancestor that shaped the tiles, and how it all led to the board you play today.

The Confucius Legend (and Why It Doesn't Hold Up)

For decades, sellers of mahjong sets in the West told a charming story: the game was supposedly created by Confucius, the Chinese philosopher, around 500 BC. The three dragon tiles were said to represent his three cardinal virtues — benevolence, sincerity and filial piety — and the game's name would come from his fondness for birds.

It's great marketing, but historians reject it. There is no record of mahjong in any Chinese text, painting or inventory before the 19th century. If the game were 2,500 years old, someone would have written about it. The real origin is much more recent — and no less fascinating.

Madiao: The Paper Ancestor

Most researchers agree that mahjong descends from Madiao (马吊), a paper card game popular in China since the Ming dynasty (1368–1644). Madiao already had features any mahjong player would recognize instantly:

  • Four players around a table;
  • Numbered suits of coins and strings of coins — which became today's circle and bamboo tiles;
  • Sets and sequences as the goal of the game.

Over the 19th century, these paper cards gained new suits, honor pieces (winds and dragons) and finally traded paper for bone and bamboo. Paper cards blew away in the wind on boats and outdoor tables; heavy tiles did not. That is how the physical format we know was born.

Fun fact: the coin suits of Madiao explain why mahjong's circle tiles are called "tong" (筒) — they depict ancient Chinese coins with a square hole in the middle.

Where Exactly Was Mahjong Born?

This is where the dispute begins. At least three Chinese regions claim the origin of mahjong:

Ningbo, Zhejiang Province

The most widely accepted theory today points to the port city of Ningbo, around 1850. The supposed creator was Chen Yumen (陈鱼门), a local official who is said to have adapted the card games of his time into bamboo-and-bone tiles, far more practical for the sailors of the harbor. Ningbo has embraced this version: the city even maintains a museum dedicated to mahjong, built in Chen's honor. In the Ningbo dialect, the word for "sparrow" sounds remarkably close to "má jiàng" — which would strengthen the city's link to the game's name.

Jiangsu and the Shanghai Region

Another school of thought places the game's birth in Jiangsu province, next door to Shanghai, where the busy trade along the Yangtze River mixed travelers, cards and gambling. The first written descriptions of the tile game by foreigners come precisely from this region at the end of the 19th century.

The Taiping Soldiers Theory

Some also argue the game spread among soldiers during the Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864), as a pastime in long encampments. This version is hard to prove, but it would help explain how quickly mahjong swept across all of China within a few decades.

Why "Mahjong" Means "Sparrow"

The game's original name, máquè (麻雀), literally means "sparrow." The most common explanation is acoustic: the clatter of tiles being shuffled on the table sounds like a flock of chattering sparrows. Over time, in northern China, the name evolved into májiàng (麻将), the form that prevailed in Mandarin. In Hong Kong and Canton the game is still called "sparrow" to this day — and it was from that Cantonese version that the West took the spelling "mahjong."

From Regional Curiosity to National Game

Between 1870 and 1920, mahjong left the tea houses of Ningbo and Shanghai and conquered the whole of China. Each region adapted the rules to its own taste, creating the variants that still exist today — an ongoing process we cover in our guide to mahjong culture and variants. The next step of that journey, the conquest of the West in the 1920s, is told in our article on the complete history of mahjong.

And here's a curious twist: the game born for four people around a table is now mostly played solo around the world. Mahjong Solitaire uses exactly the same tiles created in 19th-century China — circles, bamboos, characters, winds and dragons — but turns the contest into a puzzle of patience. It's a delightful way to get to know the original tiles without needing three more players.

What We Know (and What We Don't)

  • Certain: mahjong emerged in China in the 19th century, evolving from card games such as Madiao.
  • Very likely: the shift from cards to tiles happened along the coast between Ningbo and Shanghai, around 1850–1870.
  • Legend: the creation by Confucius and any claim of an ancient, millennia-old origin.
  • Open question: whether Chen Yumen truly "invented" the game or was simply the most famous promoter of one already in circulation.

Whichever city wins that dispute, one thing is certain: few Chinese inventions have traveled as far as these 144 little tiles. Why not feel a bit of that history yourself? Pick a board and enjoy the game!
