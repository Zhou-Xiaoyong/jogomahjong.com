How to Make a Pung in Mahjong: Three of a Kind From Any Seat

Three tiles with the same face, no order and no ends. A pung is the one meld any of your three opponents can hand you on a discard, and the one that changes your hand most when you open with it.

Three alike, four copies, one design

Three tiles, same design, same suit. None of them has a job of its own: no first, no last, no middle. A 1-2-3 of bamboo has order. A pung of 5 of circles has none.

A 136-tile set carries 34 designs at four copies each: 27 suited, four winds, three dragons. Add the eight flowers and the box reads 144, but flowers and seasons never make a set of three. They are eight different designs with one copy apiece, and the repetition a pung needs cannot happen there.

Two counts rarely get compared: 34 possible pungs against 21 possible chows. You do not pick which one to build, you wait for the set to repeat a design already in your hand. There are exactly four physical ways to assemble any pung, since the choice is three tiles out of four copies.

Classic Mahjong here plays with all 144 tiles, flowers included, and their rule does not bend: they score when they turn up and never join a meld.

The call any seat can feed

A chow can only come from the player on your left, because turns run counterclockwise and theirs is the discard that reaches you. A pung can come from any of the three, including the player on your right, who would otherwise draw next.

The name travels badly. Pong comes from Cantonese, pon is what a Japanese table says, and the move is identical.

After the call you discard without drawing from the wall. The meld sits open in front of you, the claimed tile laid sideways to mark who threw it, and those three tiles are frozen: they do not return to the hand.

The trade is blunt. You give up an unknown draw and take a tile you already know fits. In return you hand over information: the table now knows that design is spoken for, and the fourth copy is dead for everyone. For the mirror case, where only one seat can feed you, see how to make a sequence.

An open pung and a closed pung do not pay alike

Under riichi the fu table separates them cleanly.

  • Closed, simple tile (2 through 8): 4 fu.
  • Open, simple tile: 2 fu.
  • Closed, 1, 9, wind or dragon: 8 fu.
  • Open, 1, 9, wind or dragon: 4 fu.

Every kong doubles its line: 8 open, 16 closed, 32 once the tile is a terminal or an honor. The final total rounds up in tens, so a hand landing on 22 and one landing on 24 both pay 30.

Three closed pungs are worth 2 han on their own. Four are a yakuman, and that hand holds nothing but the four pungs and the pair.

A dragon pung is the cheapest reason to open

An open hand under riichi needs a yaku. Without one, even a perfect fourteen tiles cannot be declared, and that is where a dragon pung earns its keep.

A pung of white, green or red dragons is 1 han on the spot, closed hand not required. The round wind and your seat wind pay the same. A wind that is neither pays nothing.

Calling a dragon pung covers the cost of going open. Calling a 3 of bamboo pung covers nothing and also shuts the door on riichi, pinfu and iipeikou.

Hong Kong runs the same logic with fan instead of han: dragon 1 fan, round wind 1 fan, suited pung zero.

A pung wait draws from two tiles

Hold two 5 of circles and two copies remain in the set, not four. It is the shortest wait in mahjong: a pair wait reaches three, a two-sided chow wait reaches eight.

The number misleads, though. A pung accepts a discard from three players, a chow from one. Two copies open to the whole table is not the same as eight copies open to the player on your left.

Then there is the discard side. Once all four copies of a design are visible, on the table or in the pile, that design pungs for nobody. The how-to-play index shows where that count fits in a round.

The fourth copy changes the arithmetic

Open pung on the table, and the fourth copy arrives on your draw. The meld can be upgraded to a kong, with the replacement coming off the back of the wall.

The risk lands at once. The moment you declare, any opponent may take that tile and finish a hand on it. It is called chankan, and it is the most expensive answer to a player who just built a kong.

So the fourth copy stays in your hand when a rival is close to done. A kong scores no han under riichi. It pays in fu plus a draw off the back of the wall, and it hands a rival the chance to rob it. Hong Kong counts 1 fan instead. A closed pung that takes the fourth copy becomes a closed kong, worth 16 fu on a simple tile and 32 on a terminal or honor.

When a pung is worth the call

One rule covers most situations: call when the design is an honor, or when the hand is already open for another reason. Let it pass when the hand can still finish closed and the pung pays no yaku.

Count the copies before you reach out. Holding one with two already in the pile, that call is the last chance of the game and the wait is worth the price.

The exception is a hand that needs speed rather than points. Already open, clock running, opponent nearly done: every pung goes in, neutral wind included, because the goal has shifted from scoring well to finishing first. If the tiles themselves are still fuzzy, Solitaire lays all 34 designs face up on one board. And the pair completes the three melds that fill a fourteen-tile hand.
