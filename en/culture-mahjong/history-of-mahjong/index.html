History of Mahjong: from a Chinese tea house to your screen

Mahjong was born in 19th-century China and became one of the world's most beloved games. This is the history of Mahjong: where it came from, how it crossed oceans and why you can play it free in your browser today.

Where Mahjong came from

Mahjong (麻将, má jiàng) appeared in China around 1850, during the Qing dynasty, in Jiangsu province. The name joins "má" (麻, hemp) and "jiàng" (将, general) — a nod to the first hemp tiles and the game's strategic spirit. For a long time people believed Confucius invented it, but historians now point to a later origin, linked to older Chinese card games like Madiao.

The earliest tiles were bone, bamboo or ivory, each set a hand-carved work of craft. Only in the 20th century did plastic and resin make the game affordable and spread it across the world.

Did you know? A full Mahjong set has 144 tiles — almost double the 28 of a domino set.

Mahjong in imperial China

In Qing-dynasty China, Mahjong was a parlor game of well-off families and, later, of whole neighborhoods. Four players sat at the cardinal points (East, South, West, North) around a table, and a match could last all night with tea and talk. The tiles carried Chinese culture: winds, dragons, flowers and seasons.

The game spread across the country and gained different rules in each region. Cantonese Mahjong, from Hong Kong, became the "base" version the West knew. Shanghai and Sichuan Mahjong developed their own twists — more strategic or faster.

The journey west

In the 1920s, Mahjong became a craze in the United States and Europe. Joseph Babcock, an American living in Shanghai, simplified the rules and published an English manual. Mahjong clubs appeared from New York to Paris. In 1937 the American National Mah Jongg League was founded, and it still defines the rules of American Jewish Mahjong.

In Brazil, Chinese immigration — starting in Rio in 1812 and concentrating in São Paulo's Liberdade district — brought the game close to us. For decades Mahjong stayed inside Asian communities. It was the digital, English-language version that finally introduced the game to a wider audience.

The birth of Mahjong Solitaire

The Mahjong Solitaire you play here is a newer invention. It appeared in 1981 on computer floppy disks and exploded with Windows through "Mahjong Titans". Unlike the four-player game, solitaire is just you against the board: match identical tile pairs and try to clear it all, no turns, no opponents. It is the meditative side of Mahjong — Chinese zen adapted to a screen.

Today there are dozens of modes: Connect, Pyramid, Timed and more. All use the same traditional Chinese tiles, with English names so you learn as you play.

Mahjong today

Mahjong has survived two centuries because it speaks a universal language: the language of the table, of strategy and of family. From a Qing-dynasty tea house to a Brazilian coffee break, it keeps bringing people together — now also through a phone screen.
