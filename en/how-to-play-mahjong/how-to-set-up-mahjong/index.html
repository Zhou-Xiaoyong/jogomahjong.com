How to Set Up Mahjong: From the 18-Stack Wall to the First Discard

Five minutes separate an open box from the first draw, and none of it is free-form. Setup runs in six stages, each one leaning on the last, from where everyone sits to the tiles left over at the tail of the wall.

Seating comes first, before a tile moves

Someone has to be East before the walls go up, because each player builds in front of their own seat. Winds run counterclockwise: East, South, West, North. South sits on East's right, and the turn travels that way after the opening discard.

At a casual table the last winner keeps the dealership. At a fresh one, everybody rolls and the high total takes East. Settle it first for a physical reason: a wall built in the wrong spot belongs to nobody, and the whole square has to come apart.

The shuffle outlasts the build

All 144 tiles go face down in the middle and everyone pushes for a full minute. Thirty seconds leaves the four copies that formed a pung last hand sitting next to each other. That is a hand arriving half made.

Check the backs before you start. A good set has identical backs. A set assembled from two boxes has two shades of green, and a wall lying face down turns into a readable map for the player across the table.

Four rows of 18 stacks, one per player

Each player stacks 36 tiles into 18 piles of two and lines the row up in front of them. The four rows meet at the corners and close the square. The arithmetic holds: 18 × 4 = 72 stacks, 144 tiles.

The wall takes more room than people expect. A stack runs 3.5 to 4 cm wide, so 18 of them span 65 to 75 cm. Add hands and discards and a table needs 80 cm a side; at 70 cm the fourth row has nowhere to sit and somebody plays at an angle.

Build it tight. Half a centimetre of slack on one side throws the count off when the wall opens, and counting from the wrong end lands two or three tiles away from the break.

Dice pick the side, the total picks the stack

The dealer rolls three dice and counts seats from the dealer's own place in playing order until the number runs out. The seat where it stops owns the wall that gets opened. That same total then counts stacks from the right end of that wall, and the block splits there.

A Japanese table rolls two dice on the same logic with a different result: 14 tiles near the break are set aside as a dead wall and never dealt. Flower swaps and kong replacements come from that reserve, face down, for the length of the hand.

Dealing runs 4, 4, 4 and 1

From the break, the dealer takes four tiles at a time and passes the block along in playing order. Three passes of four give 12 per player, then one more tile each on the fourth pass. Thirteen in hand, 52 on the table.

The dealer does not stop at thirteen. A fourteenth tile comes off the same break, so East starts one ahead, which is the whole value of the seat and the reason that hand closes first. Deal 13 to yourself and 14 to the player on your right and the error surfaces at the declaration, not before.

Flowers draw from the far end

Draw a flower or a season, show it, park it beside the wall, draw again. That replacement does not come from the break. It comes from the opposite end, the tail of the wall, where tiles run against the direction of the deal.

Kong replacements leave from the same tail. So the wall shortens at both ends at once, and any count of who draws the last tile stops matching a clean division by four.

Five mistakes that stall a first game

  • 18 stacks from a 136 set. Eight tiles stay in the box and the square will not close. A Japanese set wants 17 stacks a side plus the 14-tile reserve.
  • Gaps at the corners. The walls have to touch. Two fingers of space between two sides turns stack counting into guesswork.
  • A 30-second shuffle. The hand arrives half built and the second round is flat.
  • Dealing the wrong way. Tiles leave the break and travel in playing order. Reverse it and the dealer draws from the wrong place for the rest of the hand.
  • Counting the hand before the flowers. Thirteen counted before the exchange become 14 after the extra draw, and that player spends the hand one tile over.

Four checks before the first discard

In this order: every side of the wall holds 18 stacks; every player holds 13 tiles after exchanging flowers; the dealer holds 14; and the break is still visible, because the coming draws leave from there.

Twelve tiles in hand means a flower swapped without a draw. Fourteen on a non-dealer means an extra tile in the deal. Neither fixes itself once the hand is moving.

Build the wall, leave the hand for the next minute

For a first game, memorize the geometry only: 18 stacks a side, a closed square, dice to choose where it opens. The deal is the part the table learns together, because everyone counts out loud at once.

The exception is the 136-tile set that shows up at Japanese tables: 17 stacks a side plus 14 tiles held back. Build 18 there and the wall runs long while the hand stalls short.
