Mahjong in Brazil: Why the First Move Is Choosing the Rules

Somebody opens the box on the dining table, spills 144 tiles and asks whether you play with flowers. The person across from you says their set has no flowers at all. Nobody has drawn yet, and there are already two different games on the table.

The open box before the first draw

You stack four rows and somebody asks whether a flower scores. The answer comes back flat: this house set has no flowers, it holds 136 tiles, and the missing eight went into a dead wall. No draw yet, and the argument is already about the rulebook.

That is how mahjong usually starts in Brazil. Nobody buys an annual card in Portuguese or checks a national ranking before sitting down, so rules get settled as conversation before they turn into arithmetic.

Two arrivals, two sets

In 1812 the ship Vulcano carried roughly 200 Chinese tea growers from Macau to Rio de Janeiro, with seedlings for the royal farm where the Botanical Garden sits today. No record says whether a set of tiles came along.

On August 15, 1900 the steamer Malange docked at Santos with 119 Chinese men between 20 and 40, listed in the old Immigrants' Hostel register, now the Immigration Museum. On June 18, 1908 the Kasato Maru tied up at the same port with 165 families and 786 people.

That second ship opened Japanese immigration and brought riichi with its 136 tiles. The Chinese community carrying the other set arrived in numbers much later.

The wave that brought a table

Chinese migration picked up in the 1950s: people from southern China, Hong Kong and Macau after 1949, then organized migration from Taiwan in the 1960s, faster after 1971. Current estimates put about 300,000 Chinese people in Brazil, 90 percent of them in the state of São Paulo.

Liberdade was a Japanese neighborhood before it became a Chinese landmark; on Galvão Bueno and Conselheiro Furtado the shop signs changed ahead of the tiles. The Cantonese community that ran São Paulo's street-market pastel stalls kept mahjong tables going in associations and living rooms.

Learn from a nikkei grandmother and you count yaku. Learn from an aunt from Guangdong and you count fan.

144, 136, or 152 tiles

Counting the box settles half the argument:

  • 144 tiles: 108 suited, from three suits numbered 1 through 9 in four copies each; 28 honors, four winds and three dragons in four copies each; and 8 bonus tiles, flowers and seasons. This is the Hong Kong set and the Chinese competition set.
  • 136 tiles: the same 34 types in four copies, with no flowers. Riichi sets aside 14 in a dead wall for replacements and the dora indicator.
  • 152 tiles: the standard 144 plus eight jokers. This is the American game, with no chows and a hand printed on that year's card.

Imported sets sold in Brazil are almost always the 144 kind. Your home table started on Chinese rules without anybody voting on it.

What actually changes

The gap is not vocabulary. Hong Kong wants 3 fan; European competition sets the floor at 8 points, where four pungs and a pair add up to only 6. Riichi asks a different question: does this hand have a yaku? Dora multiplies, it does not qualify.

Discards change with it. If you already threw away the tile that would complete your hand, riichi lets you win only on your own draw, never on somebody else's discard. That is furiten, and it lasts the whole hand. Under Chinese competition rules, a chow can be claimed only by the player to the discarder's right; a pung or a kong is open to anyone.

The character comes before the score

At a mahjong workshop run by a Brazilian university Confucius Institute, the class started by separating the traditional and simplified forms of the character tile suit, then moved to the five calls of the table: chow, pung, kong, ready, and the win. Learning in Portuguese, the tile is the first hurdle.

No card and no ranking

In the United States the national league has published its card since 1937, each edition running from April 1 through March 31. In Europe the association founded on June 25, 2005 holds 21 national bodies together and keeps one ranking across borders; Portugal's is the only Portuguese-speaking member.

Brazil has neither. Rules get agreed at the table, which is why a house rule here is normal procedure, not an exception. Every new group starts the same conversation over.

Where the game shows up

The easiest tables to find still hang off a date and a family. Municipal Law 17.197 of 2019 put the Moon Festival on São Paulo's calendar, and Chinese New Year keeps gathering people. At the Liberdade street fair the tiles turn up more often as shop goods than as a game in progress.

Search for mahjong on a Brazilian phone and you rarely land at a four-player table.

A third door: solitaire

Mahjong Solitaire here runs on 112 tiles in 56 pairs, with no opponent at all. A tile can only be picked if nothing sits on top of it and one long side is clear. No discards, no fan to argue over, no fourth player.

It is a different game, but it teaches reading faces fast and spotting what is blocked. The site's Classic mode, at 144 tiles, is the closest thing to a Chinese table.

What to check before buying

  • Count the tiles: 144, not 136 or 152.
  • Look for eight flowers and seasons, and four copies of every wind.
  • Check whether the suits carry corner numerals; without them, a beginner memorizes more.

Where to start

Start with a 144-tile set on Hong Kong rules and 3 fan. It is the set you can actually buy here, it allows chows, it needs no annual card, and four people can finish an evening without a scoring spreadsheet.

The exception is a group already playing riichi. Forget 3 fan there and learn yaku and furiten first, since 136 tiles and a dead wall change the count from end to end. The European floor is in Mahjong in Europe, the annual card in Mahjong in the United States, and the Culture index holds the rest.
