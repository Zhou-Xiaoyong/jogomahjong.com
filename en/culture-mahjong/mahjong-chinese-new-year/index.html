Mahjong and Chinese New Year: the game behind the celebration

Chinese New Year and Mahjong have walked side by side for over a century: on family tables, the clack of the tiles is the soundtrack of the holiday. Here is where that tradition comes from and how it reached Brazil.

Why Mahjong became the game of Chinese New Year

In the lunar calendar, Chinese New Year — also called the Spring Festival (春节, Chūnjié) — is the biggest family celebration in Chinese culture. And in almost every home that takes the tradition seriously, the Mahjong table holds the same place of honor as the turkey at a Western Christmas. It is no exaggeration: for many families, the clatter of tiles is the true soundtrack of the holiday.

The reason is simple. Mahjong is a four-player game played around a table, with conversation, tea and snacks passed hand to hand. It is exactly the right format for a festival built to gather three generations of relatives. That is why it became the favorite pastime of Chinese New Year evenings — and you can explore more of this cultural connection in our Mahjong Culture hub.

The tradition was born on the tables of imperial China

Mahjong emerged in the late 19th century, during the Qing dynasty, and it was born as a parlor game. In the cities of southern China, families would open their bamboo-and-ivory sets during the long holidays, and the New Year — with its weeks of rest — was the perfect moment. To see how the game took shape in that period, read about Mahjong in the Qing dynasty, when the fall of the empire and the rise of the republic fixed many of the rules we use today.

Back then, playing during the holiday was more than fun: it was a ritual of socialization. Elders taught their grandchildren, neighbors knocked on the door for "one quick game", and the winner of the year's first match was hailed as if they had brought good news for everyone.

The luck of the first win and the noise of the tiles

There is an old belief: winning the first Mahjong game of the new year attracts luck for the next twelve months. That is why nobody wants to lose the "opening victory". Many families even leave an empty chair at the table — legend says it is for the god of fortune, who plays beside the invisible family.

The noise has a purpose too. Mahjong tiles are made to click loudly when slapped on the table; the sound is considered auspicious, as if it scared away the spirits of bad luck. No wonder the clatter of tiles became a symbol of celebration. For those who enjoy the game's origins, it is worth revisiting the full history of Mahjong and seeing how these superstitions weave into the invention of the tiles themselves.

What gets played during the holiday

  • Traditional Cantonese Mahjong — the four-player version most common in family celebrations.
  • Games for symbolic sweets or cash — the prize matters less than playing together.
  • Neighborhood tournaments — in many districts the whole block sets up tables in the street for the holiday.

Chinese New Year in Brazil, with Mahjong on the table

In Brazil, the tradition arrived with Chinese immigration and took firm root in São Paulo, especially in the Liberdade district. Every year during the lunar holiday, the community raises tents, parades dragons and — of course — opens Mahjong tables for anyone who wants to learn. It is common to see grandmothers teaching Brazilian grandchildren to match the suits, a cultural bridge that mixes Portuguese with the Chinese characters on the tiles.

How to celebrate Chinese New Year with Mahjong

You do not need an ivory set or four available relatives to honor the tradition. Mahjong solitaire — the version where you match pairs of tiles until the board is clear — carries the same spirit of pause and focus. It is the perfect way to mark the turn of the year when family is far away or the table is busy with dinner.

Facts about the holiday

  • Chinese New Year lasts 15 days, and Mahjong shows up in almost all of them.
  • Red is the color of luck — which is why many tables use a red cloth under the tiles.
  • In Hong Kong it is common to gift Mahjong sets for weddings and milestone birthdays.
  • The "God of Wealth" (Caishen) is often shown playing or holding tiles.
  • Some families keep a special set just for the holiday, used once a year.
