What Mahjong Characters Mean: How to Read the Tiles

The five of characters carries 五 on top and 萬 below, which reads as "five ten-thousand" rather than a plain five. Learning the rest of the writing takes less time than learning to score a hand.

Sixteen characters cover the whole set

A full set runs to 144 tiles. Twenty-eight are honors you identify from a single character: four winds and three dragons, four copies apiece. Another 36 make up the wan family, where the character fills the entire face. The remaining 80, between bamboo, circles and the bonus tiles, are read by picture and need no writing at all.

Add it up and the reading load is sixteen characters. Nine numerals, four directions, three ideas.

The 萬 at the bottom of every numbered character tile

Wan tiles are built in two registers. The number sits on top. Underneath, the same 萬 repeats on all nine. The character 萬 (wàn) means "ten thousand," which is why a five of characters is closer to "five ten-thousands" than to a bare five. The suit's logic is money counted in myriads, the way large sums were tallied in nineteenth-century China.

The numerals are plain and mostly straight strokes: 一 (yī), 二 (èr), 三 (sān), 四 (sì), 五 (wǔ), 六 (liù), 七 (qī), 八 (bā), 九 (jiǔ). If you have seen the eight of characters, you have seen 八 over 萬 and nothing else.

Early export sets sometimes swap the numerals for the formal banking forms, 壹 貳 參 instead of 一 二 三. Those forms existed to make ledgers hard to forge, and a few workshops carried the habit onto tiles. If your three looks like a knot of strokes, that is the reason.

Four gets avoided, eight gets favored

四 (sì, four) sits close to 死 (sǐ, death). The tone differs, the ear still catches it, and some players will not leave the four of characters as a final discard when a safer tile is available. 八 (bā, eight) leans toward 發 (fā, to prosper) and carries the opposite charge. None of this changes a rule. It changes the mood at the table, and no rulebook prints it.

The sou suit is not bamboo

Here is where most beginners go wrong. Sou tiles are drawn as bundles of sticks that look like bamboo, and English-language sites often call the suit Bamboo. The character 索 (suǒ) means "cord." The original image was a string of copper coins, a thousand to the string, which is how cash actually traveled. Bamboo attached itself to the name because the tiles were cut from bamboo in the first place, and the resemblance did the rest.

The one of sou shows no stick at all. It shows a bird, usually a sparrow on a branch. The Chinese name of the game, máquè, means sparrow, and the bird stayed on the tile as a signature.

Pin tiles are coins, face up

The circle suit needs no decoding. Each 筒 (tǒng) is a copper coin with its square hole, seen straight on. The one of pin is a single large coin; from two to nine they stack in columns. No hidden symbol, no abstraction. It is money drawn plainly.

The four winds

東 (dōng) east, 南 (nán) south, 西 (xī) west, 北 (běi) north. These are the easiest characters in the set to memorize, since each appears on four identical tiles and never mixes with a numeral. In four-player mahjong your seat wind scores higher than the other winds when you pung it, so identifying your own wind before the first discard pays off later.

中, 發, and the tile with nothing written on it

中 (zhōng) means center. 發 (fā) means to issue or to prosper, shortened from fācái, to get rich. 白 (bái) means white, and it trips up more newcomers than any other tile, because plenty of sets print no character on it at all: an empty frame, sometimes a plain blue rectangle. Nothing is missing. The blank is the design.

Why the same tile looks different in another set

Mainland China simplified a batch of characters in the 1950s and 1960s. 萬 became 万, 東 became 东, 發 became 发. Sets made on the mainland afterward may show the simplified forms; sets from Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan keep the traditional ones, which are also what most digital games reproduce. Neither version is a misprint.

Outside China it drifts further. Japanese riichi tiles use manzu, pinzu and souzu, and add a red five in each suit, the akadora, worth extra points. A Japanese five of manzu and a Chinese 五萬 are the same tile under very different scoring rules.

Putting the characters to work

On a screen the character stops being decoration and becomes a shortcut. Mahjong Solitaire gives you no labels: you glance at 中, 發 and 白 and already know what pairs with what. Early on, nearly all the time you lose hunting for matches goes into honor tiles, which are exactly the ones with writing on them.

The order that works is three dragons, four winds, nine numerals. Half an hour and the stalling stops. Flowers and seasons can wait; they are eight bonus tiles that rarely decide anything.

Details almost nobody mentions

  • A standard set holds 144 tiles: 108 suited, 16 winds, 12 dragons, 8 bonus.
  • Every tile exists four times, dragons included, so a pung still leaves one copy loose in somebody's hand.
  • The name mahjong comes from máquè, the Mandarin word for sparrow, not from any "house of hemp" story that circulates online.
  • Sets shipped to Europe and the Americas in the early twentieth century often carried a small Arabic numeral in the corner for buyers who could not read Chinese.
  • The wan family is the only suit where you read the value instead of counting it in the artwork.

Learn sixteen characters and the game opens up

If you memorize one group, make it the 28 honor characters. They are the tiles that force decisions: no sequences, only pairs and pungs, so they sit in your hand waiting for a partner. The exception is solitaire play, where telling 中 from 白 matters more than any scoring rule, because the board rewards fast recognition and asks for no strategy.
