Who Invented Mahjong? The Real History Behind the Game

Mahjong's origin is wrapped in myth. In this article we separate legend from fact and explain what historians actually know about who created China's most famous game.

The most famous legend: Confucius and the four winds

If you ask a casual player "who invented Mahjong?", the most common answer is: Confucius. The legend says the Chinese philosopher created the game around 500 BC, and that the four winds of Mahjong (East, South, West, North) represented the directions of his travels or the seasons of the year. It is a beautiful story and it appears in the oldest rule books that reached the West.

The problem is that there is no historical evidence for it. Confucius lived more than two millennia before any record of Mahjong, and the first written accounts of the game only appear in the 19th century. Scholars believe the Confucius legend was a marketing device used by the first tile factories in the early 20th century, to give the product an aura of ancient wisdom. It worked: the story crossed time and many people still swear it is true.

What historians actually say

Among researchers, there is a consensus that Mahjong had no single inventor. It evolved gradually from much older Chinese card games, taking the form of bone, bamboo and clay tiles only in the 19th century. Here are the two most accepted explanations:

From card suits to Mahjong (the Madiao game)

In the 16th century, Chinese players already enjoyed Madiao (马吊), a card game whose suits vaguely resemble those of modern Mahjong: characters, bamboo sticks and circular coins. Over roughly 300 years, those cards were replaced by physical tiles — more durable, more tactile and easier to shuffle on a tea table. It is exactly this transition that gives rise to the "tile game" we know today.

The Ningbo theory and Chen Yumen

The most solid hypothesis places the birth of Mahjong in the city of Ningbo, in Zhejiang province, around 1870. An official named Chen Yumen (陳魚門) is said to have adapted local card games into a tile game, adding the wind and dragon suits that now define Mahjong. Other accounts credit the invention to army officers in Shanghai. The common point is clear: all of them place the game in the late Qing dynasty, not in remote antiquity.

Why there is no official "inventor"

Unlike chess or tennis, Mahjong grew as a popular parlor game, passed from table to table without any official record. Each region adapted the rules, and nobody patented the game. When it finally reached the West in the 1920s, it already carried dozens of regional versions. With no owner and no record, the "authorship" became legend — and the legend chose Confucius.

In practice, Mahjong is accumulated cultural heritage: many hands, many cities and many generations contributed to the game you play today, including our own Mahjong Solitaire, built for English-speaking players to enjoy for free in the browser.

How the game conquered the world (and reached Brazil)

In the 1920s, the American Joseph Babcock lived in Shanghai, simplified the rules and published an English manual. Mahjong became a craze in the United States and Europe, with clubs, books and luxury sets. In Brazil, it arrived through the Chinese communities of São Paulo, especially in the Liberdade neighborhood, where it is still a family game during Chinese New Year. 5 facts about the origin of Mahjong

  • The name "Mahjong" probably comes from "má" (hemp) and "jiàng" (general) — a reference to the first natural-fiber tiles and the strategic nature of the game.
  • The earliest tiles were made of bone, bamboo and ivory; ivory sets were a status symbol in the early 20th century.
  • Before becoming a tile game, Mahjong was played with paper cards for centuries.
  • The Confucius legend was widely spread by manufacturers to boost the product in the American market.
  • In Brazil, digital Mahjong Solitaire is the most common entry point to the game — even for people who did not know its name.
